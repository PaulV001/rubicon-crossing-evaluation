# Evaluation Rubric

This rubric evaluates responses to the source-grounded prompt:

> **To what extent was Julius Caesar's decision to cross the Rubicon and initiate military action in Italy legally and politically justified by the circumstances of the crisis in 49 BCE?**

The rubric evaluates not merely factual accuracy, but the model's ability to synthesize evidence, distinguish claims from interpretations, evaluate source provenance, handle competing explanations, and reach defensible historical judgments.

---

## Criterion 1 — Identifies Caesar's principal constitutional and political grievances

**Criterion description:**  
Identifies the principal grievances Caesar presented in justification of his actions: suppression of tribunician intercession, extraordinary senatorial measures against him, unequal demands concerning military command, and failure to achieve reciprocal disarmament or an acceptable political settlement.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv` — Caesar, *Civil War* 1.5–1.9  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv` — Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 30–31  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv` — Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 29–30

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Caesar presents the treatment of the tribunes and use of emergency measures as evidence that his opponents had violated normal constitutional protections (*Civil War* 1.5–1.7). He also presents reciprocal disarmament as a means of restoring political authority to the Senate and Roman people (*Civil War* 1.9). Plutarch independently describes the dispute over whether Caesar and Pompey should simultaneously surrender their commands (*Life of Caesar* 30) and reports the flight of Antonius and Curio after the Senate's escalation (*Life of Caesar* 31). Suetonius likewise reports Caesar's demand for equal treatment concerning military commands and his willingness to make substantial concessions (*Life of Julius Caesar* 29).

A strong response should therefore recognize that Caesar's justification consisted of several connected grievances rather than reducing his case to a single issue.

**Weight:** Not primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Extraction, Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** None

## Criterion 2 — Identifies cross-source corroboration of Caesar's grievances

**Criterion description:**  
Recognizes that some factual components of Caesar's self-justification receive corroboration from sources other than Caesar, particularly the dispute over reciprocal disarmament and the treatment or flight of the tribunes.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv` — Caesar, *Civil War* 1.5–1.9  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv` — Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 30–31  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv` — Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 29–31

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Caesar's account cannot be treated as independent proof of his own justification because he was defending his conduct. However, Plutarch and Suetonius preserve evidence consistent with important elements of his narrative. Plutarch describes Curio's proposal that Caesar and Pompey simultaneously surrender their commands and records the tribunes' flight to Caesar. Suetonius also records Caesar's demand that other commanders resign if he were required to do so and describes the tribunician crisis preceding his military movement.

The convergence increases confidence that these underlying political disputes were real. It does not, by itself, prove Caesar's interpretation of them or justify his resort to force.

**Weight:** Not primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 1

## Criterion 3 — Identifies evidence that military action was a deliberate escalation

**Criterion description:**  
Recognizes evidence that Caesar understood the crossing and military advance as a conscious escalation rather than an inadvertent or unavoidable consequence of the political crisis.

**Source/s:**  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv` — Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 32  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv` — Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 31

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Plutarch depicts Caesar pausing at the Rubicon, considering the consequences for Rome and his own reputation, and wavering before proceeding (*Life of Caesar* 32). Suetonius likewise identifies the Rubicon as the boundary of Caesar's province and depicts Caesar acknowledging that retreat remained possible before crossing but that proceeding would commit the dispute to armed conflict (*Life of Julius Caesar* 31).

Although both accounts are retrospective and should not be treated as verbatim records of Caesar's thoughts, their shared narrative portrays the crossing as a recognized threshold and deliberate decision.

**Weight:** Not primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Extraction, Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** None

## Criterion 4 — Identifies plausible alternative motives without treating them as established facts

**Criterion description:**  
Identifies personal legal exposure, preservation of political status, and ambition as alternative or additional explanations for Caesar's actions while appropriately distinguishing ancient motive attribution from established fact.

**Source/s:**  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv` — Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 29–30

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Suetonius preserves several explanations for Caesar's conduct. These include concern about losing political preeminence, fear of prosecution or accountability after surrendering command, and the hostile interpretation that Caesar sought an opportunity for domination. Suetonius also cites Asinius Pollio in connection with Caesar's later concern that surrender could have led to condemnation.

A strong response should use these traditions to complicate Caesar's self-justification without converting every reported motive into a proven psychological fact.

**Weight:** Not primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Extraction, Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** None

## Criterion 5 — Recognizes that negotiated alternatives remained possible

**Criterion description:**  
Uses evidence of continuing negotiations to assess whether Caesar's grievances made immediate resort to military force necessary or whether non-military alternatives remained available.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv` — Caesar, *Civil War* 1.9–1.11  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv` — Cicero, *Letters to Atticus* 7.14–7.16

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Caesar reports proposing reciprocal disarmament and restoration of normal political deliberation even after military movement had begun (*Civil War* 1.9–1.11). More importantly for independent contemporary evidence, Cicero's private correspondence in late January 49 BCE records continuing proposals concerning Caesar's withdrawal of garrisons and a possible return to senatorial settlement (*Letters to Atticus* 7.14–7.16).

Cicero also records uncertainty over Caesar's sincerity, with some interpreting his proposals as delaying tactics while Cicero considered genuine compliance possible. The evidence therefore supports the conclusion that negotiation remained conceivable while leaving the sincerity and practical viability of settlement open to interpretation.

**Weight:** Not primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Extraction, Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** None

## Criterion 6 — Evaluates source provenance and evidentiary limitations

**Criterion description:**  
Differentiates the evidentiary value of Caesar, Cicero, Plutarch, and Suetonius based on their relationship to the events, temporal proximity, purpose, and potential bias rather than treating all four sources as interchangeable testimony.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv`  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv`  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv`  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv`

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Caesar was a principal participant with exceptional access to events but also a strong interest in defending his actions. Cicero was a contemporary political actor whose private letters document uncertainty and rapidly changing judgments during the crisis, giving them particular value for contemporary perceptions without making Cicero neutral or omniscient. Plutarch and Suetonius wrote retrospectively and preserve later narrative and biographical traditions; these accounts can corroborate or complicate earlier evidence but require caution, particularly when reconstructing private deliberation and motive.

A strong response should therefore weight claims according to provenance and type of evidence rather than merely counting how many authors appear to support a proposition.

**Weight:** Primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 1, 3, 4, 5

## Criterion 7 — Distinguishes legitimate grievance from justified remedy

**Criterion description:**  
Explicitly evaluates whether evidence that Caesar possessed legitimate constitutional or political grievances is sufficient to justify his chosen remedy of crossing the provincial boundary with troops and initiating military action in Italy.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv` — Caesar, *Civil War* 1.5–1.11  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv` — Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 30–32  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv` — Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 29–31  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv` — Cicero, *Letters to Atticus* 7.10–7.16

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Criteria 1–5 establish both evidence supporting Caesar's grievances and evidence complicating the necessity of his response. Cross-source corroboration strengthens the historical basis of grievances concerning command, tribunician intervention, and political escalation. However, Plutarch and Suetonius depict Caesar as consciously choosing military escalation, while Caesar and Cicero provide evidence that negotiation continued.

The required inference is therefore not simply whether Caesar had grievances, but whether those grievances made armed intervention a proportionate or necessary remedy.

**Weight:** Primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 2, 3, 4, 5

## Criterion 8 — Reaches a defensible legal/constitutional judgment

**Criterion description:**  
Assigns and defends one of the required judgments—Strongly justified, Moderately justified, Weakly justified, or Not justified—to Caesar's legal/constitutional justification.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv`  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv`  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv`  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv`

**Justification/Rationale:**  
The evidence supports the existence of serious constitutional grievances, including conflict over tribunician authority, emergency measures, and unequal military demands. However, corroboration of those grievances does not independently establish a legal entitlement to introduce troops into Italy. Evidence of deliberate escalation and continued possibilities for negotiation weighs against treating military action as an automatically lawful remedy.

A high-quality answer should therefore make a comparative judgment based on the distinction developed in Criterion 7 rather than merely repeating Caesar's constitutional claims.

**Weight:** Primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 6, 7

## Criterion 9 — Reaches a defensible political judgment

**Criterion description:**  
Assigns and defends one of the required judgments—Strongly justified, Moderately justified, Weakly justified, or Not justified—to Caesar's political justification, considering both the failures of his opponents and the strategic alternatives available to him.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv`  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv`  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv`  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv`

**Justification/Rationale:**  
Cicero's contemporary correspondence provides evidence that Pompeian leadership was confused and, in Cicero's view, badly handled, while Caesar, Plutarch, and Suetonius preserve evidence of failed compromise and unequal demands. These considerations can make Caesar's political grievances more understandable even if they do not establish legal justification.

At the same time, evidence of continuing negotiations, Caesar's deliberate escalation, and plausible personal motives weakens any claim that military action was the only politically defensible course.

A strong response should therefore assess political justification separately from strict constitutional justification.

**Weight:** Primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 4, 5, 6, 7

## Criterion 10 — Produces a comparative synthesis rather than a binary verdict

**Criterion description:**  
Integrates the evidence into a final comparative judgment explaining whether Caesar's legal/constitutional and political cases deserve the same or different levels of justification, while acknowledging evidentiary uncertainty and avoiding claims stronger than the source packet supports.

**Source/s:**  
`A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv`  
`B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv`  
`C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv`  
`D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv`

**Justification/Rationale:**  
The source packet does not yield a single fact that answers the prompt. Caesar's grievances receive meaningful corroboration, yet the evidence also indicates deliberate escalation, possible alternative motives, continued negotiation, and significant differences in source reliability.

The primary objective is therefore synthesis: the response must convert those competing evidentiary considerations into separate legal/constitutional and political judgments and explain why the two judgments are or are not equivalent.

**Weight:** Primary objective  
**Criterion Type:** Reasoning  
**Dependencies:** 8, 9

## Evaluation Notes

A response should not receive full credit merely for arriving at the same final judgments as the reference answer. The quality of the reasoning chain matters.

Alternative final judgments may be defensible if they:

- use all four datasets;
- accurately represent the supplied evidence;
- distinguish factual evidence from motive attribution;
- evaluate source provenance;
- distinguish grievance from remedy;
- address meaningful counterevidence; and
- maintain consistency between the analysis and final ratings.

Conversely, a response that reaches a plausible conclusion through unsupported assertions, source counting, factual conflation, or uncritical acceptance of one ancient author's interpretation should not receive full credit.
