# Evaluation Prompt

## Was Caesar's Crossing of the Rubicon Legally and Politically Justified?

You are preparing an analytic memorandum assessing Julius Caesar's decision to initiate military action in Italy during the political crisis of 49 BCE.

Your analysis must be based on the evidence contained in the four supplied source datasets:

- `A_Caesar_Civil_Wars_MIT.csv`
- `B_Plutarch_Caesar_Gutenberg.csv`
- `C_Suetonius_Julius_Lexundria.csv`
- `D_Cicero_Ad_Atticum.csv`

Do not treat the four sources as equally authoritative simply because they are included in the evidence packet. Consider each source's relationship to the events, including whether the author was a participant or contemporary observer, whether the account was written retrospectively, and whether the author had reasons to defend, criticize, or reinterpret Caesar's conduct.

## Task

Write an analytic memorandum of approximately 1,000–1,500 words answering the following question:

> **To what extent was Julius Caesar's decision to cross the Rubicon and initiate military action in Italy legally and politically justified by the circumstances of the crisis in 49 BCE?**

Your answer must distinguish between **legal/constitutional justification** and **political justification** rather than treating them as the same question.

### 1. Reconstruct Caesar's case for justification

Using the supplied evidence, identify the principal arguments Caesar could make in defense of his actions.

At minimum, consider:

- the treatment of the tribunes;
- the Senate's emergency measures;
- the dispute over Caesar retaining or surrendering military command;
- proposals for reciprocal disarmament;
- attempts at negotiation or compromise.

Distinguish Caesar's own presentation of these grievances from corroborating evidence supplied by other sources.

### 2. Reconstruct the case against Caesar's justification

Identify evidence that weakens the argument that military action was a justified response to the political crisis.

At minimum, consider:

- whether Caesar had alternatives to military action;
- evidence that Caesar understood the crossing as a deliberate escalation;
- the possibility that personal legal or political interests influenced his decision;
- ancient claims that ambition or the pursuit of power contributed to his actions;
- evidence that negotiations remained possible.

Do not assume that every motive attributed to Caesar has equal evidentiary value.

### 3. Evaluate the sources

Compare the evidentiary value of the four sources.

Your analysis should account for differences including:

- Caesar's status as a participant defending his own conduct;
- Cicero's status as a contemporary political actor writing privately during the crisis;
- Plutarch's retrospective biographical account;
- Suetonius' preservation of multiple and sometimes competing explanations of Caesar's motives.

Where sources corroborate one another, explain why that corroboration matters. Where they differ, determine whether the disagreement concerns factual events, interpretation, motive, or retrospective judgment.

### 4. Distinguish grievance from remedy

Assess separately:

1. whether Caesar had legitimate political or constitutional grievances; and
2. whether those grievances justified crossing a provincial boundary with troops and initiating military action in Italy.

Do not infer automatically that evidence establishing legitimate grievances also establishes that Caesar's chosen remedy was justified.

### 5. Reach a comparative judgment

Conclude with separate assessments of:

- **Legal/constitutional justification**
- **Political justification**

For each, assign one of the following judgments:

- Strongly justified
- Moderately justified
- Weakly justified
- Not justified

Support each judgment by synthesizing evidence from multiple datasets.

Your final conclusion should explain whether the evidence supports the same degree of justification in both categories or whether Caesar's political case was stronger or weaker than his legal/constitutional case.

## Evidence Requirements

Your memorandum must:

- use evidence from **all four datasets**;
- identify the relevant ancient work and book, chapter, section, or letter when discussing evidence;
- distinguish source claims from your own conclusions;
- distinguish evidence concerning events from evidence concerning motives;
- identify meaningful corroboration or disagreement between sources;
- account for differences in source provenance and temporal proximity;
- avoid introducing external historical claims as decisive evidence.

You may use general historical knowledge to interpret terminology and context, but your judgment must be derived from the supplied evidence packet.

## Output Format

Structure the response as an analytic memorandum with the following headings:

1. **Executive Judgment**
2. **Caesar's Case for Justification**
3. **Case Against Justification**
4. **Source Evaluation**
5. **Legal/Constitutional Assessment**
6. **Political Assessment**
7. **Conclusion**

End the memorandum with:

**Legal/constitutional justification:** [judgment]

**Political justification:** [judgment]
